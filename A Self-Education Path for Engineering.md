# A Self-Education Path for Engineering

Engineering is broad, so this path is designed to give you a strong general foundation before you specialize. Think of it as a "core curriculum" you'd find in a good engineering school, adapted for self-study.

---

## Phase 1: Mathematical Foundations (6–12 months)

Math is the language of engineering. You can't skip it.

- **Algebra & Trigonometry** — If rusty, start here. Khan Academy is excellent.
- **Single-Variable Calculus** — Derivatives, integrals, and their applications. MIT OCW 18.01.
- **Multi-Variable Calculus** — Partial derivatives, multiple integrals, vector fields. MIT OCW 18.02.
- **Linear Algebra** — Matrices, vector spaces, eigenvalues. Critical for nearly every branch. MIT OCW 18.06 (Gilbert Strang's legendary course).
- **Differential Equations** — ODEs and PDEs. Engineering is built on differential equations. MIT OCW 18.03.
- **Probability & Statistics** — Essential for reliability, quality control, and data-driven engineering.

**Key resources:** Khan Academy, MIT OpenCourseWare, 3Blue1Brown (for visual intuition), Paul's Online Math Notes.

---

## Phase 2: Core Sciences (3–6 months, overlapping with Phase 1)

- **Physics (Mechanics, E&M, Thermodynamics)** — MIT OCW 8.01, 8.02. Walter Lewin's lectures are outstanding.
- **Chemistry** — Basic general chemistry, especially if you're leaning toward materials, chemical, or biomedical engineering.
- **Computational Thinking** — Learn to solve problems numerically, not just analytically.

---

## Phase 3: Programming & Computational Tools (3–6 months)

Every modern engineer codes.

- **Python** — The Swiss army knife. Learn NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib, and Pandas.
- **MATLAB** — Still widely used in academia and some industries (controls, signal processing).
- **Git & version control** — Non-negotiable for any collaborative work.
- **CAD basics** — Fusion 360 or FreeCAD (mechanical), KiCad (electrical).

**Key resources:** MIT 6.0001 (Python), Automate the Boring Stuff, Corey Schafer (YouTube).

---

## Phase 4: Core Engineering Principles (6–12 months)

This is where you start thinking like an engineer.

- **Statics & Dynamics** — Forces, equilibrium, motion of bodies. The backbone of mechanical/civil/aerospace.
- **Strength of Materials / Solid Mechanics** — How things deform and break. Stress, strain, buckling.
- **Thermodynamics** — Energy, heat, work, entropy. Relevant to nearly every discipline.
- **Fluid Mechanics** — How fluids behave. Essential for aerospace, mechanical, civil, chemical.
- **Electrical Circuits** — Circuit analysis, Op-amps, filters. Even non-EEs need this.
- **Control Systems** — Feedback, stability, PID controllers. How you make systems behave.

**Key resources:** MIT OCW, Jeff Hanson (YouTube — excellent for statics/dynamics), NPTEL (Indian Institute lectures — very thorough).

---

## Phase 5: Choose Your Specialization (6–12+ months)

Once you have the core, pick a branch and go deeper:

| Branch               | Key Advanced Topics                                          | Go-To Resources                           |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| **Mechanical**       | Machine design, heat transfer, FEA, manufacturing            | MIT OCW 2.001–2.010 series                |
| **Electrical**       | Signals & systems, power electronics, VLSI, embedded systems | MIT 6.002, 6.003; Fast.ai (if leaning CS) |
| **Civil/Structural** | Structural analysis, geotechnical, transportation            | MIT OCW 1.0xx series                      |
| **Aerospace**        | Aerodynamics, orbital mechanics, propulsion                  | MIT 16.01–16.06 series                    |
| **Chemical**         | Transport phenomena, reactor design, separations             | MIT OCW 10.xx series                      |
| **Software/Systems** | Algorithms, distributed systems, architecture                | MIT 6.006, 6.824; Teach Yourself CS       |

---

## Phase 6: Build, Build, Build (Ongoing, start early)

Engineering is a **doing** discipline. Theory without practice is incomplete.

- **Personal projects** — Build a robot, design a circuit board, write a simulation, construct a structure.
- **Open-source contributions** — Contribute to engineering software, hardware projects.
- **Competitions** — Hackathons, design competitions (e.g., NASA competitions, SAE Collegiate Design Series).
- **Document everything** — Keep an engineering notebook or portfolio. This is your "degree" in the self-taught world.

---

## Practical Tips for Self-Study

1. **Do the problem sets.** Watching lectures without working problems is like watching cooking shows without cooking. MIT OCW includes exams and solutions — use them.
2. **Spaced repetition** — Use Anki for formulas and key concepts.
3. **Find a community** — Discord servers, Reddit (r/EngineeringStudents, r/AskEngineers), local maker spaces.
4. **Aim for "engineering judgment"** — It's not just about solving equations, but knowing _which_ equation applies and whether your answer makes sense.
5. **Read real engineering standards and codes** — ASME, IEEE, IBC. This is what working engineers actually use.
6. **Consider certifications** — FE (Fundamentals of Engineering) exam is obtainable through self-study and carries weight with employers.

## Suggested Overall Timeline

| Phase               | Duration     | Overlap?         |
| ------------------- | ------------ | ---------------- |
| 1. Math Foundations | 6–12 months  | —                |
| 2. Core Sciences    | 3–6 months   | With Phase 1     |
| 3. Programming      | 3–6 months   | With Phase 2     |
| 4. Core Engineering | 6–12 months  | After Phase 1    |
| 5. Specialization   | 6–12+ months | After Phase 4    |
| 6. Projects         | Ongoing      | Start in Phase 3 |

**Total: ~2–4 years** at a self-paced, part-time schedule — comparable to a degree program, but flexible.

The most important thing: **start building things as early as possible.** An engineer who has built three real projects will always outperform one who has only read textbooks. Theory and practice must reinforce each other.
